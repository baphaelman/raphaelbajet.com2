Single Cycles

Scramble a Rubik's Cube. How often can a blindfolded solver fix every edge in one unbroken chain of swaps?

No cycle break22.65%
No break, no flips13.74%

Look closer and both are made of e: they're e/12 and e/12. Euler's number, hiding in a Rubik's Cube!

Press → to step through like slides, or scroll. Drag the cube to turn it.

Twelve edges

A cube has 26 visible pieces: 6 centers, 8 corners and 12 edges. The centers never move relative to each other, so they fix each face's color.

This article is only about the edges. The corners are grayed out from here on.

Every edge has a home

On a solved cube, each edge sits in one particular slot: its home. Slots are named by the two faces they touch. UF is between Up and Front. DB is between Down and Back.

Twelve slots, twelve names. Three of them, BL, DL and DB, are on the back. Drag the cube to see them.

A scramble

A scramble moves edges away from home. In this one, seven edges have moved and five are still home.

Each piece still knows where it belongs from its colors. The yellow-green edge belongs in DF wherever it is.

The buffer

The beginner method for blindfolded solving is Old Pochmann. One slot, UR, is the buffer.

You look at the piece in the buffer, work out where it belongs, and swap it there using one memorized algorithm. Here the buffer holds the yellow-green edge, so the first swap is with DF.

One swap, up close

The swap is an algorithm called the T-perm: fourteen face turns that swap whatever is in UR with whatever is in UL. It also swaps two corners, shown in color on this slide so you can watch them.

Our first swap is with DF, so a solver first makes two setup turns, D' L2, that bring the edge in DF up to UL without touching the buffer. Then the T-perm, then the setup turns undone. Every swap drags the same two corners along, but Old Pochmann fixes the corners in a separate pass afterwards (with one extra algorithm if the number of edge swaps was odd).

So for our purposes, the buffer can be swapped with any edge at will, and the corners can be ignored.

press → to play the T-perm, then again to undo the setup

Swap, swap, swap

Each swap sends one piece home and brings a new piece into the buffer. The swap with DF brought in the piece that belongs in FL, so you repeat with whatever arrives: FL, then UB.

The arrows point from each piece to its home. They form a loop, and every swap removes one arrow.

press → once for each swap

The loop closes

After three swaps, the buffer's own white-red piece arrives back in UR. Four edges were solved in three swaps. A loop like this is called a cycle.

But UF, DR and BR are still wrong. They form a cycle of their own, and the buffer isn't in it. Swapping the buffer with itself does nothing, so the method is stuck.

A cycle break

To get unstuck, you deliberately swap the solved buffer piece into the other cycle, then follow that cycle until the buffer piece comes back. Solvers call this a cycle break.

These three edges took four swaps. Had they been part of the buffer's cycle, they would have taken three. Each break costs an extra swap and an extra thing to memorize.

press → to make the break, then again to finish it

Every scramble is a set of cycles

Finish the solve, then rewind it swap by swap back to the scramble, and draw an arrow from every misplaced piece to its home. Follow the arrows from any piece and you always come back to where you started. So the arrows always split into closed loops.

This scramble has two: four edges through the buffer, and three without it.

Flatten it

For counting, the cube's shape doesn't matter, only which slot points to which. So peel the twelve slots off the cube and lay them in a ring, keeping the arrows.

The question

Call a scramble a single cycle when one loop covers every edge that's out of place. Then the solve needs no cycle break.

Ours has two loops, so it doesn't count. Press → to see one that does: a single loop through eight edges, with the other four home. On a random scramble, how often does this happen?

First, a wrinkle: flips

An edge can be home but flipped, sitting in its slot with its colors swapped. Is that edge solved? Not quite.

A cube can never have exactly one flipped edge. Every move flips an even number of edges, so flips come in pairs.

So there are two answers to find: how often the solve needs no cycle break, and how often it needs no break and no flip algorithm.

press → to flip a second edge

The buffer's cycle

A solver always traces from UR. So the question is: does the buffer's cycle cover every misplaced edge?

The way in is to sort scrambles by the length of the buffer's cycle and handle each length on its own. Nothing about the cube forces that split; it's a choice. But it's the choice that makes counting easy: a fixed length means a fixed number of arrows to follow out of UR, and every other edge simply has to be home.

A neat fact helps: the buffer's cycle is equally likely to have any length from 1 to 12. Follow the arrows out of UR. The first can point to any of the 12 homes, and only one of them, UR's own, closes the loop at once. Each later arrow has one home fewer to choose from, and the chances cancel down to 112 for every length.

The strip at the bottom is sampling random scrambles live. Every bar settles at 112.

press → to follow each arrow

The answers

If the buffer's cycle has length k, the other j=12−k edges must all be home, with chance 1/j!. The ring steps through a few lengths. Dropping k=1, the solved cube:

112∑j=0101j!≈e12≈22.65%

That sum is the series for e. It's the chance of no cycle break, about 2 scrambles in 9.

A flipped edge sitting at home doesn't cause a break, but it does need a separate flip algorithm. Requiring that there are none adds 2j to each term, which gives the series for e:

112∑j=0101j!2j≈e12≈13.74%

That's the chance the edges are just one clean cycle from the buffer, with no break and no flip algorithm.

My first attempt

Where I first went wrong

When I first wrote this up, I answered a slightly different question: how often do the edges form a single cycle anywhere, with the solved cube counted too?

That isn't what a solver needs. A single cycle that skips UR, like this one, still needs a cycle break, and nobody is handed a solved cube to solve. Claude pointed this out while building this page; the answers above are the corrected ones.

The original counting is still a nice piece of combinatorics, so here it is.

Counting cycles anywhere

The edges can be in any of 12! arrangements, all equally likely. (A real cube can't swap just two edges, but the corners take up the difference, so the edges alone reach all of them.)

The trick is the same as before: sort single cycles by their length k and count each length separately. It's a choice, not something the cube imposes, and it's what makes the count manageable. With k fixed, a single cycle is just two independent choices.

A single cycle of length k takes two choices. Which edges: (12k) ways, with the rest at home. What order: start at one edge and follow the arrows. The other k−1 edges appear in some order, giving (k−1)! cycles.

Three edges make exactly (3−1)!=2 cycles: the same triangle, traveled one way or the other.

First answer

Divide by 12! and most of the factorials cancel:

(12k)(k−1)!12!=1k(12−k)!

Every edge left out of the cycle has to happen to be home, which (12−k)! makes unlikely fast: 112 for 12 edges, 111 for 11 (the edge left out can be any of the 12), under 2% by 9. Add every length, plus a lone 1/12! for the solved cube:

112!+∑k=2121k(12−k)!=103447414720≈24.94%

Full: adding flips back

With flips, there are 12!×211 edge states: the first 11 edges can each be flipped or not, and the 12th is forced to keep the count even.

Edges outside the cycle must be home and unflipped. Edges inside can be flipped in any even-sized pattern: choose freely for k−1 of them and the last is forced. That's 2k−1 patterns. The cube is cycling through all 8 for this 4-edge cycle.

First answer: counting flips

Dividing by 211 as well, each term picks up one more factor:

1k(12−k)!212−k

Each edge left out of the cycle now has to be home and unflipped, a further 1-in-2 chance. The 12-edge cycle stays at 112, but the 11-edge cycle halves and shorter ones shrink faster.

112!211+∑k=2121k(12−k)!212−k≈14.40%

Those were my original answers: a cycle anywhere, with the solved cube counted. They're within a couple of points of the real ones, 22.65% and 13.74%, but they answer a question no solver asks.

Check all four

The stage is generating random edge states, each a uniformly random permutation with a random even set of flips, and sorting them under all four definitions: the two real answers and the two from my first attempt. The bars settle onto the exact answers well before the run stops at a million.
